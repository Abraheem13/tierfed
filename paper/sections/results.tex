\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

The results proceed from the protocol checks to the main comparison, the conditions under which per-tier scheduling can pay, the cost on constrained links and privacy. Unless stated otherwise, values are means over seeds on Diabetes with the MLP and the specialty sites, $p$-values are from paired Wilcoxon tests, and tables S-$n$ are in the supplement.

\subsection{Main Comparison}
\label{sec:results-main}
At the validation-selected round the federated methods are close (Table~\ref{tab:main}). FedPer reaches \num{auFedperAuroc}, and apart from AdaComm no method differs from it by more than \num{auFedSpread} AUROC, below the 0.005 that this study calls relevant (largest difference: \num{auFedSpreadName}, $p\num{auFedMinP}$). AdaComm is lower by a relevant margin, \num{auAdacommEightDFedper} ($p\num{auAdacommEightDFedperP}$), at \num{auAdacommEightMb} against \num{auFedperMb}~MB, and local training by \num{auLocalDFedper} ($p\num{auLocalDFedperP}$), so federation helps on this task.
\ifflag{auTierfedAlwaysFourFour}{On this benchmark \tierfed{} chooses $\mathbf K=(4,4)$ in every seed, and after warm-up its schedule is that of the fixed period $(4,4)$ round for round\ifflag{auTierfedEqualsFixed}{, so the two rows are identical to the last digit}{}. }{}%
This is what Proposition~\ref{prop:cuberoot} predicts: at the end of warm-up $\Psi=\num{auGateSpecialtyPsi}$\%, so no per-tier schedule can improve the drift surrogate over a uniform period by more than that fraction. \tierfed{} is non-inferior to FedPer at $\Delta_{\mathrm{NI}}=0.01$ ($p\num{auTierfedNiTenP}$) and at 0.005 ($p\num{auTierfedNiFiveP}$), with \num{auTierfedMb} against \num{auFedperMb}~MB over 60 rounds; AdaComm, at \num{auAdacommEightMb}~MB, is non-inferior at 0.01 ($p\num{auAdacommEightNiTenP}$) but not at 0.005 ($p\num{auAdacommEightNiFiveP}$).

The lead of the sparse schedules in earlier versions of this work is a property of the round at which they were read. Full-rate training passes its best validation round early (round \num{auFedperRound} on average, against \num{auTierfedRound} for \tierfed{}; dots in Fig.~\ref{fig:dyn}(a)) and then overfits, while a sparse schedule climbs in a sawtooth that peaks after each synchronisation. Over the last ten rounds the sparse schedules are therefore ahead (\tierfed{} \num{auTierfedLast}, FedPer \num{auFedperLast}); at the validation-selected round the lead disappears, while the upload gap of Fig.~\ref{fig:dyn}(b) remains. Fine-tuning each site's final model for three local epochs \ifflag{auFtAlwaysLowers}{lowers the AUROC of every method}{does not raise the AUROC of every method}, by \num{auFtLossMin} to \num{auFtLossMax}, so the late-round lead is not local adaptation either: sparse synchronisation overfits later. LAG's earlier lead came from its weighting: its final-round AUROC is \num{auLagUniformFinal} with the equal weights used there and \num{auLagFinal} with sample weights, level with FedAvg (\num{auFedavgFinal}); at its default threshold it never skips an upload.

AUPRC follows the same pattern (FedPer \num{auFedperAuprc}, \tierfed{} \num{auTierfedAuprc}, local training \num{auLocalAuprc}, prevalence baseline \num{diabetesmlpPrevalenceFrac}). Inverse-frequency class weighting shifts every method's scores, so raw ECE is \num{auEceRawMin}--\num{auEceRawMax}; Platt scaling on each site's validation split lowers it to \num{auEceCalMin}--\num{auEceCalMax} for every method at no communication cost. The worst site's AUROC is \num{auWorstMin}--\num{auWorstMax}: the sample-weighted mean hides sites at which no method is useful.

At the same budget (Table~\ref{tab:dp}), every schedule that releases before its selected round is far below local training, which spends no budget: the best reaches \num{auDpBestReleasing}, against \num{auDpLocal}, and round by round no releasing schedule rises above \num{auDpCurveMax} (Fig.~\ref{fig:resdp}(c)). One-shot federation appears to match local training, but \ifflag{auDpOneshotPreRelease}{in every seed}{in some seeds} its selected round (round \num{auDpOneshotRound} on average) comes before its single release; after the release its AUROC is \num{auDpOneshotFinal}. It is local training from a common initialisation, not federation. With 20 sites the noise on the average is $zC/N$ per release, and at $\varepsilon^\star=4$ it swamps the update whatever the schedule, while a share of \num{auDpFedperClip} of FedPer's updates is clipped at $C=1$. The exact accountant (\ref{eq:gdp}) would lower $z$ by \num{gdpSigmaCutFourPct}\% for every method alike, which does not close a gap of this size. At this federation size the result is therefore not a ranking of schedules but a recommendation: do not federate, federate hundreds of sites, or protect records instead of sites.
