\subsection{Main Comparison}
\label{sec:results-main}
At the validation-selected round the federated methods are close (Table~\ref{tab:main}). FedPer reaches \num{auFedperAuroc}. Apart from AdaComm, no method differs from it by more than \num{auFedSpread} AUROC, below the 0.005 that this study calls relevant; the largest difference, for \num{auFedSpreadName}, has $p\num{auFedMinP}$. AdaComm is the one federated method that is lower by a relevant margin, \num{auAdacommEightDFedper} ($p\num{auAdacommEightDFedperP}$), at \num{auAdacommEightMb} against \num{auFedperMb}~MB. Local training without communication differs by \num{auLocalDFedper} ($p\num{auLocalDFedperP}$), so federation helps on this task.
\ifflag{auTierfedAlwaysFourFour}{On this benchmark \tierfed{} chooses $\mathbf K=(4,4)$ in every seed, and after warm-up its schedule is that of the fixed period $(4,4)$ round for round\ifflag{auTierfedEqualsFixed}{, so the two rows are identical to the last digit}{}. }{}%
This is what Proposition~\ref{prop:cuberoot} predicts: at the end of warm-up $\Psi=\num{auGateSpecialtyPsi}$\%, so no per-tier schedule can improve the drift surrogate over a uniform period by more than that fraction. \tierfed{} is non-inferior to FedPer at $\Delta_{\mathrm{NI}}=0.01$ ($p\num{auTierfedNiTenP}$) and at 0.005 ($p\num{auTierfedNiFiveP}$), with \num{auTierfedMb} against \num{auFedperMb}~MB over 60 rounds; AdaComm, at \num{auAdacommEightMb}~MB, is non-inferior at 0.01 ($p\num{auAdacommEightNiTenP}$) but not at 0.005 ($p\num{auAdacommEightNiFiveP}$).

The lead of the sparse schedules in earlier versions of this work is a property of the round at which they were read. Over the last ten rounds the sparse schedules are ahead (\tierfed{} \num{auTierfedLast}, FedPer \num{auFedperLast}), because full-rate training passes its best validation round early (round \num{auFedperRound} on average, against \num{auTierfedRound} for \tierfed{}) and then overfits; at the validation-selected round the lead disappears. Fine-tuning each site's final model for three local epochs \ifflag{auFtAlwaysLowers}{lowers the AUROC of every method}{does not raise the AUROC of every method}, by \num{auFtLossMin} to \num{auFtLossMax}, so the late-round lead is not local adaptation either: sparse synchronisation overfits later. LAG's lead in the earlier table has a different cause. With the equal weights used there its final-round AUROC is \num{auLagUniformFinal}; with sample weighting it is \num{auLagFinal}, level with FedAvg (\num{auFedavgFinal}), and at its default threshold it never skips an upload, so the lead came from the weighting.

AUPRC follows the same pattern (FedPer \num{auFedperAuprc}, \tierfed{} \num{auTierfedAuprc}, local training \num{auLocalAuprc}, prevalence baseline \num{diabetesmlpPrevalenceFrac}). Inverse-frequency class weighting shifts every method's scores, so raw ECE is \num{auEceRawMin}--\num{auEceRawMax}; Platt scaling on each site's validation split lowers it to \num{auEceCalMin}--\num{auEceCalMax} for every method at no communication cost. The worst site's AUROC is \num{auWorstMin}--\num{auWorstMax}: the sample-weighted mean hides sites at which no method is useful.

\subsection{Peak Load, Radio Wake-Ups and Link Cost}
\label{sec:results-net}
\label{sec:results-emu}
A mean quota leaves the payload of a single round untouched. Every transmitting round of the aligned $(4,4)$ period sends the full shared payload, so on the 5-Mbit/s link \num{auPeakFixedFourFourViolSix} of all rounds exceed a window of 0.6 times the full-payload upload time (Fig.~\ref{fig:au}(d)). Staggering the two tiers keeps the mean and caps every post-warm-up round at \num{auPeakFixedFourFourStPost} of the payload, so only the warm-up rounds exceed that window (\num{auPeakFixedFourFourStViolSix} of rounds); with the warm-up sent at eight bits the share is \num{auPeakFixedFourFourStWEightViolSix}. The AUROC changes by \num{auStaggerDiff} ($p\num{auStaggerP}$). The price is paid in radio wake-ups (Section~\ref{sec:netmodel}): each site transmits in \num{auPeakFixedFourFourStWake} rounds instead of \num{auPeakFixedFourFourWake}. A uniform period has one tier and cannot shape the peak this way; on this benchmark, this is what tier structure adds.

Over 60 rounds on the cell-edge link, communication takes \num{auNetFedperTime}~s for FedPer and \num{auNetTierfedTime}~s for \tierfed{} with independent links, and \num{auNetFedperShared}~s against \num{auNetTierfedShared}~s when the sites share one uplink, where bytes convert one-to-one into time. Replaying the per-site uploads of the earlier runs over TCP paced at the model's rate matched the model to within \num{emuMaxAbsRelErrPct}\% (supplement), which tests its implementation, not its realism.

Table~\ref{tab:dp} compares the DP-eligible schedules at the same budget with full participation. Every schedule that releases before its selected round is far below local training, which spends no budget: the best of them reaches \num{auDpBestReleasing}, against \num{auDpLocal} for local training on the same splits. One-shot federation appears to match local training, but \ifflag{auDpOneshotPreRelease}{in every seed}{in some seeds} its selected round (round \num{auDpOneshotRound} on average) comes before its single release; after the release its AUROC is \num{auDpOneshotFinal}. It is local training from a common initialisation, not federation. With 20 sites the noise on the average is $zC/N$ per release, and at $\varepsilon^\star=4$ it swamps the update whatever the schedule, while a share of \num{auDpFedperClip} of FedPer's updates is clipped at $C=1$. The exact accountant of Section~\ref{sec:method-privacy} would lower $z$ by \num{gdpSigmaCutFourPct}\% for every method alike, which does not close a gap of this size. At this federation size the result under client-level DP is therefore not a ranking of schedules but a recommendation: do not federate, or federate hundreds of sites, or protect records instead of sites.
