\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{What the evidence supports.} Three claims are supported by ten-seed paired tests on patient-deduplicated data. First, on this benchmark synchronising less does not cost accuracy; every sparse schedule matches or slightly exceeds full-rate personalisation, and \tierfed{} is non-inferior to FedPer on AUROC and AUPRC at a fraction of the upload. Second, timing and message-size reductions compose multiplicatively when compressors act on the update with error feedback, giving more than two orders of magnitude less upload at unchanged accuracy. Third, under client-level DP the number of transmitting rounds is the dominant design variable: round-sparse methods are charged a smaller budget and inject noise fewer times, and they retain substantially more utility at the same noise multiplier. A communication quota is therefore also a privacy quota, which matters operationally because the two are usually negotiated with different stakeholders.

\textbf{What it does not support.} \tierfed{} is not more accurate than simpler round-sparse alternatives. A fixed uniform period of four rounds is the most accurate configuration we ran, and AdaComm reaches comparable accuracy with fewer bytes when DP is off. The divergence-driven mechanism that motivates per-tier intervals rarely activates on this data; at most quotas the exact solver returns the same interval for both shared tiers. Under DP, \tierfed{} and AdaComm are level at moderate noise, \tierfed{} is better at $\sigma=2$, and AdaComm is better at $\sigma=4$. The case for \tierfed{} over these alternatives is operational rather than statistical: it exposes a quota that maps directly onto a slice or window allocation, solves for the schedule exactly, and composes with compression and DP accounting, whereas AdaComm exposes a loss-driven period and a fixed schedule exposes nothing an operator can set from a byte budget.

\textbf{A retraction.} An earlier version of this work reported that fixed multi-rate schedules lose about 0.05~AUROC to personalised baselines. With one encounter per patient, patient-grouped splits and the fallback-tier bug removed, that result does not reproduce. Readers who relied on it should not.

\textbf{Limitations.}
(i) The sites of the tabular benchmark are specialty buckets with a share cap, not hospitals; a benchmark with true hospital identifiers (eICU) is supported by the released code but was not run for this version because it requires credentialed access.
(ii) The bound is for SGD and the main benchmark uses Adam; the SGD arm shows that the ordering of methods depends on the local solver.
(iii) $\hat\Gamma_g$ is a dispersion proxy that scales with the interval that produced it; the controller is a feedback loop on that proxy whose stability we observe but do not prove.
(iv) The network model omits scheduling, fading and retransmission, and its profiles are representative targets rather than measurements.
(v) The inversion attack is generic, the DP budgets are large, and neither should be read as a privacy guarantee.
(vi) Full participation is assumed; partial participation would add a sampling term to (\ref{eq:bound}) and permit subsampling amplification in (\ref{eq:rdp}).
(vii) The FT-Transformer arm was trained with a non-deterministic GPU attention kernel; all other results are bit-reproducible.
(viii) On the imaging benchmark under strong label skew, the methods that share the classifier head are the most accurate and both head-private methods trail them; the results here do not transfer to that setting.
(ix) Ten seeds on one public tabular dataset and one imaging collection is a benchmark, not a clinical validation.

\textbf{Practical guidance.} For a tabular model of this size, an operator who needs a byte budget should set $\rho$ from the transfer window through (\ref{eq:compose}) and (\ref{eq:netcost}), compose the schedule with 8-bit quantisation or sparse ternary compression with error feedback, and, when client-level DP is required, prefer the sparsest schedule that meets the accuracy target, because each transmitting round is charged against the privacy budget.
